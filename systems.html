HOW THE IDEAS GET BETTER

Make the hint stick

A practical way to collect observations, blend ideas, check the useful point and turn them into pictures that say what we mean.

Build an image brief
Big pictures. Plain talk. A useful bloody hint.

Keep the good gag. Give it a proper brief.

AI can produce another picture quickly. Getting the right picture takes a clear idea, useful source material and a few deliberate choices. This workflow gives the people making Rough as Guts a shared way to talk about the work.

Catch the moment

Start with something someone saw, did or had to clean up after. Write the observation in ordinary language. Capture where it happened, who was involved, what made it funny and what you wished had happened instead.

  • Keep the original observation.
  • Separate what happened from what you are assuming.
  • Name the habit or decision at the centre of the joke.
  • Choose the audience who will recognise it.

Find the useful point

Say what the reader should understand or do after seeing the page. Keep it small enough to picture. Where the subject involves health, safety or a formal procedure, identify the relevant source and a person able to review it.

  • One main point per image or clearly separated panel.
  • A visible action the reader can recognise.
  • A factual source when the subject needs one.
  • An unresolved question recorded as a question.

Try a few jokes, then blend with purpose

Explore different ways to make the same point: a literal exaggeration, an unexpected comparison, a familiar excuse, a before-and-after scene or a short exchange. Take the strongest parts from different versions when they work together.

  • Compare several captions against the same useful point.
  • Choose the joke that works for the intended reader.
  • Keep the clearest scene and the best line together.
  • Drop extra ideas when they muddy the picture.

Write the image before generating it

Describe the picture in concrete terms: who is present, what each person is doing, where the action happens and which props matter. State the reading order for panels and make the important action easy to see.

  • Specify character variety without requiring one lead character across every edition.
  • Describe body position, gaze, hands and object placement where they affect meaning.
  • Give each panel its own scene and purpose.
  • Set aside clear space for captions and labels.
  • List visual mistakes that would change the meaning.

Review the result against the brief

Look at the image before letting the joke carry you away. Check the action, equipment, expressions, panel sequence and relationship between words and picture. A funny illustration can still communicate the wrong thing.

  • Can a reader tell what is happening without an explanation?
  • Does the picture show the intended action and consequence?
  • Are important objects, hands and directions plausible?
  • Does every caption match its panel?
  • Has any invented lettering or detail changed the point?
  • Does it suit the audience and setting?

Keep the version worth reusing

Save the observation, chosen copy, image brief, references, review notes and finished asset together. When adapting it for a poster, book page or animation, check that the central point still survives. A smaller format may need a simpler idea.

  • Record the source and the changes made.
  • Give each version a clear status.
  • Keep unresolved review points visible.
  • Recheck the adapted format before calling it finished.

More clue. Better results.

The underlying aim is greater competence: noticing more, understanding the situation and choosing an action that works. A laugh can make that conversation easier. The useful habit is what people take away.

Knock an idea into shape
