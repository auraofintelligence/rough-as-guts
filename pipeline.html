THE ROUGH AS GUTS PRODUCT SHED

Out of the shed

A good yarn could become a cartoon, a sticker, a skit, a club mascot or something stuck to the back of a dunny door. Here are some things worth trying.

Explore the product rangeTry the weird stuff
Big pictures. Plain talk. A useful bloody hint.

A book is one way out of the shed.

A missing 10 mm socket could carry a single-panel joke or a whole detective series. A camp-fridge argument could become a newsletter column, a short skit or a poster written by the fridge itself. The trick is to give each version a reason to exist and make a small one before building a mountain of them.

Open the other shed.

Things to try around ag shows, country halls, roadhouses, camp recreation rooms and club days. These are creative starting points for a local version.

The Cubicle Gazette

A tiny changing newspaper for the back of the door, complete with a headline, one cartoon and an absurd classified ad.

A serious broadsheet front page reporting that the last clean mug has been sighted near the sink.

Where & how to try it

Country-show toilets, club bathrooms and camp amenities, with permission from the venue.

First test: One readable sheet with a single joke. Test the lettering and whether people understand it without leaning forward.

The Lost Property Royal Show

Give abandoned everyday objects the treatment of prize livestock, with judging notes and ribbons.

A magnificent unmatched glove under a Champion ribbon; a line of hopeful coffee mugs waits to be judged.

Where & how to try it

An ag-show display or club foyer.

First test: Create a handful of removable illustrated award cards and a mock display from fictional objects.

The Corrugated Comic

A comic designed across a series of upright corrugated panels, so readers walk along the story.

A three-stage attempt to close a stubborn gate, ending with the quiet discovery that somebody stacked timber behind it.

Where & how to try it

A show stand, shed exhibition or temporary community-art display.

First test: Make a paper model and one removable panel before producing an outdoor version.

Moppa's Feral Chores Club

A proudly scruffy emu becomes an accidental ambassador for finishing jobs.

Moppa insists a mess is a creative project while sweeping it into the unmistakable shape of a second emu.

Where & how to try it

A family activity day, junior club session or illustrated home edition.

First test: One character sheet, a short strip and a simple activity page. Check that the joke works for the intended age group.

The Roadhouse Placemat Yarn

A one-meal comic, caption puzzle or small 'spot the forgotten item' scene that can be read while food arrives.

A traveller packed everything for the trip except the reason they stopped: the shopping list still pinned to the fridge at home.

Where & how to try it

A proposed roadhouse or club-meal tabletop trial.

First test: One paper placemat design, tested around plates and cups at actual size.

The Ten Millimetre Escape Room

A short picture puzzle follows a missing socket through a ute, shed and kitchen drawer.

Comic evidence cards, an unhelpful witness statement and a toolbox with a suspiciously comfortable empty space.

Where & how to try it

A club social day, library activity or ag-show stall.

First test: One printable puzzle with a clear solution and no special props.

The Shed Choir

A performed comedy piece builds a familiar shed soundtrack from voices, ordinary props and increasingly unhelpful commentary.

A line of solemn performers each holds a caption card while one person makes the noise of the tool everyone claims to own.

Where & how to try it

A hall gig or club variety night.

First test: A short read-through or audio demo with simple stage directions.

The Town's Unofficial Useful Map

Map the landmarks everyone really uses: the shady seat, the shop door nobody knows how to open and the bend where visitors always turn too early.

Start with one. Let the next one earn its place.

01

One camp gripe, three useful formats

  1. Capture a real shared-fridge story and its best line.
  2. Make one poster written from the fridge's point of view.
  3. Adapt it into a short newsletter letter.
  4. Use the same characters in a brief skit, with a proper ending.

First test: Show the poster at the size and distance intended for a noticeboard. Ask what habit it is about before discussing whether it is funny.

02

A recurring town cartoon

  1. Choose a fictional town and a small stable cast.
  2. Write three independent stories from ordinary local frustrations.
  3. Finish one single-panel cartoon and one short strip.
  4. Prepare a clean sample package for an editor to assess.

First test: Print the cartoon at a normal newspaper column width and confirm that the smallest lettering is still comfortable to read.

03

A club mascot that does some work

  1. Find the club habit members recognise and can laugh about.
  2. Create an original mascot with a repeatable personality.
  3. Make a match-day gag, a volunteer thank-you and a practical notice.
  4. Revise with the club before considering stickers or merchandise.

First test: Put the three examples together and see whether members recognise the same character and voice in each.

04

Turn a good spread into a small product family

  1. Finish a short picture-book sequence around one recognisable habit.
  2. Select a single visual gag that still works outside the story.
  3. Try it as a sticker or print with text resized for that object.
  4. Make an animatic only if timing adds another laugh.

First test: Read the book spread aloud, then show the stand-alone design without its story. Both versions should make sense on their own.

05

Illustration attached to real task material

  1. Start with the actual task, its current document and the responsible reviewer.
  2. Agree exactly which steps or controls need visual support.
  3. Draw the real equipment and setting with editable labels.
  4. Review the images against that job, then retain the source and revision record.

First test: Ask the task team to walk through one drawing and identify anything missing, wrong or open to a dangerous interpretation.

From a yarn to a finished thing

  1. Catch the real habit

    Save the story, the exact line and the small detail that makes it recognisable.

    Keep: A short seed note with the source and the intended useful point.

  2. Choose one home for it

    Pick the place it will be encountered and the time it gets from the reader.

    Keep: One format and a definite first audience context.

  3. Make the smallest complete version

    Finish one spread, poster, strip, sticker sheet or short scene with readable lettering.

    Keep: A thing people can actually react to.

  4. See what people get

    Show it in the intended size. Ask what happened, where the laugh was and what point stayed with them.

    Keep: Specific changes to the joke, sequence, picture or type.

  5. Revise and check the point

    Keep the strongest joke, fix confusing artwork and check factual claims against suitable sources. Task-based material needs the relevant task owner and reviewer.

    Keep: An approved version with dated source notes and the final text.

  6. Release the versions that earn their keep

    Adapt the approved idea to a few useful sizes and contexts, then keep feedback with the source.

    Keep: A small set of finished files, with the original and derivatives clearly identified.
