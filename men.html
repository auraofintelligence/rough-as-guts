For the blokes

Dating, dinner, fitness, parenting and travel, plus the fine art of being useful before someone has to ask three times.

Have a lookMake a new page
Big pictures. Plain talk. A useful bloody hint.

A few more clues for the bloke who’ll give anything a crack.

For the mate who can rebuild an engine but cannot find the laundry basket. For the dad who says he’s “babysitting” his own kids. For the bloke who has never met a job he couldn’t begin before reading the instructions. There is plenty to work with.

Dating: bring your ears

A proposed guide to manners, listening, respectful boundaries and enjoying someone’s company without performing a one-man tribute to yourself. The funny bit is the gap between how charming you think you are and what the other person is actually experiencing.

  • Ask a question. Let the answer finish.
  • Make plans that consider both people.
  • Take a clear answer seriously.
  • Leave room for kindness, nerves and an ordinary human conversation.

Exercise: the sequel matters

The first workout makes a fine story. A routine you can return to is more useful. These concepts explore overdoing the grand entrance, skipping the basics and buying enough equipment to open a very small gym.

  • Learn what the movement is supposed to look like.
  • Choose an effort you can build on.
  • Make room for recovery and a realistic schedule.
  • Let progress be a reason to keep going.

Cooking, cleaning and hygiene: participate in the civilisation

Feed yourself. Clean up what you use. Notice the jobs that appear to do themselves because somebody else keeps doing them. The proposed pages turn ordinary domestic friction into jokes people can recognise without needing a family argument first.

  • A meal includes what happens to the kitchen afterwards.
  • The washing basket is a location, not a theory.
  • Replace the thing you finished.
  • Personal hygiene travels with you to work, dates and shared rooms.

Parenting: you’re on the team

The proposed parenting material finds humour in lost hats, missing forms, bedtime negotiations and adults who pack for themselves before checking the child’s bag. Useful habits start with paying attention and sharing responsibility.

  • Know the plan and the important details.
  • Take ownership of a whole task.
  • Give a child your attention without a running commentary from your phone.
  • Make room for another parent’s approach and the child’s actual needs.

Travel: being ready is surprisingly relaxing

From a weekend on the island to a longer trip, the gag is usually hiding in a missing booking, an impossible suitcase or someone who thinks local rules are suggestions addressed to other people.

  • Check the destination, timing and essentials.
  • Pack for the trip you are taking.
  • Share the planning and the carrying.
  • Treat hosts, staff and fellow travellers like people.

The gift angle

Give it to your brother, partner, dad or mate with affection and a fairly straight face. Leave room for him to point at a page and say, “That’s you.” The best version of this book can survive being passed around the table.

  • Every topic on this page is in development.
  • Sample gags show the intended tone and are not finished products.
